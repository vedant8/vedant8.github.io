      <i class="fas fa-ghost" style="margin-right: 0.35rem;"></i> Cosmic Ghost Stories
    </span>
    <h2 style="margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 0.75rem;">Why Do We Build a Telescope Out of Antarctic Ice?</h2>
    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      Right now, roughly <strong>100 trillion neutrinos</strong> are passing straight through your body every single second. They have almost no mass, carry zero electric charge, and barely interact with ordinary matter at all. A single neutrino could fly through a solid wall of lead a light-year thick without slowing down.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
      That stubbornness makes them terrible for easy detection, but phenomenal cosmic messengers. While light gets blocked by interstellar dust and cosmic rays get bent by magnetic fields, neutrinos rocket straight from violent cosmic accelerators right to Earth. To catch just a handful of them, we turned a cubic kilometer of crystal-clear glacial ice at the South Pole into the world’s strangest particle detector.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      Science is way too fun to keep locked inside journal articles. I love giving interactive talks for high school classrooms, university clubs, planetariums, and local community groups about how we hunt invisible particles from deep space.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
      Instead of drowning audiences in equations, I focus on the detective story behind multimessenger astronomy: how combining light, gravitational waves, and neutrinos lets us see the Universe in completely new ways.
    </p>

    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      Nothing sparks curiosity faster than putting real detector hardware in someone’s hands. During lab tours, science festivals, and student workshops, I bring out optical sensors, circuit boards, and 3D-printed prototypes so visitors can see how a physics idea becomes an instrument.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
      We talk through the messy, creative side of experimental engineering: how you test sensitive electronics on a lab bench, troubleshoot weird signals, and build equipment tough enough to survive extreme cold.
    </p>

    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      Whenever I mention working at the South Pole, the first questions are rarely about particle cross-sections. People want to know what it feels like to step off a ski-equipped cargo plane at minus 40 degrees, how you sleep when the sun never sets, and what happens when a screwdriver drops in the snow.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
      Sharing behind-the-scenes stories from Antarctic field seasons is one of my favorite ways to show students that astrophysics isn’t just staring at code on a monitor—it’s a team adventure across the globe.
    </p>
