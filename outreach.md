      <i class="fas fa-ghost" style="margin-right: 0.35rem;"></i> Ghosts from the Cosmos
    </span>
    <h2 style="margin-top: 0; margin-bottom: 0.75rem;">The Strangest Telescope in the World</h2>
    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      Right now, roughly <strong>100 trillion neutrinos</strong> are passing straight through your body every single second. They have almost no mass, carry zero electric charge, and barely interact with ordinary matter at all. A single neutrino could fly through a solid wall of lead a light-year thick without slowing down.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
      That shyness makes them very difficult to detect, but incredible for mapping out the furthest reaches of the Universe! While light gets blocked by interstellar dust, and charged particles get deflected by stray magnetic fields, neutrinos barrel towards us directly from wherever they were born. To see even one of the trillions hitting the Earth, we used a billion tons of glacial ice at the South Pole to build a telescope like no other.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>

  <div style="flex: 2 1 380px;">
    <h3 style="margin-top: 0; color: #157878;"><i class="fas fa-tools" style="margin-right: 0.4rem;"></i> Chasing Ghosts at the South Pole</h3>
    <p style="margin-top: 0;">
      The IceCube Neutrino Observatory is a tour de force of big science in extreme environments. Built over 7 seasons of construction, IceCube consists of over 5000 optical sensors buried in a gigaton of glacial ice.
    </p>
    <p style="margin-bottom: 0;">
     Finding neutrinos is like hunting for a needle in a stack of needles, with IceCube detecting thousands of particles every second created in charged particle collisions in the atmosphere, known as cosmic ray air showers. The cosmic neutrinos we are most interested in, however, are far more rare, with about ten detected every month.
    </p>
  </div>
</div>
